NuCube Tests Pump-Free Microreactor Heat at Utah's San Rafael Lab

What's actually happening at San Rafael

As first reported by Interesting Engineering, advanced-nuclear developer NuCube Energy has started a new round of non-nuclear trials at the San Rafael Energy Lab in Emery County, working alongside Brigham Young University researchers. The team is using a 250-kilowatt induction furnace to electrically simulate the thermal conditions inside its planned NuSun reactor, without any radioactive fuel on site. That's a meaningful detail for anyone trying to size up the timeline: this is hardware validation, not reactor operation.

The design itself skips the pumps, pressure vessels, and plumbing that define conventional nuclear plants in favor of sealed heat pipes that move thermal energy by basic physics. CEO Cristian Rabiti called the new test platform a way to achieve "meaningful validation of energy-conversion and industrial-heat applications" ahead of commercial deployment. Lower part count generally means lower manufacturing cost and a lighter regulatory lift, which is the whole investment thesis behind heat-pipe reactors as a category.

Why this matters for industrial land, not just energy policy

The stated targets for this technology are clean hydrogen synthesis and chemical production, two sectors that need continuous, very high heat that solar and wind can't reliably deliver on their own. If NuCube or a competitor eventually commercializes this, it changes the site-selection math for heavy industrial users who have historically been tied to natural gas infrastructure or grid interconnection queues that stretch years. A reactor that can be factory-built and sited near the point of use is a different proposition than waiting on a transmission upgrade.

Emery County isn't a market most Wasatch Front investors track closely, but it's exactly the kind of rural industrial geography that could see new interest if this technology matures: existing energy infrastructure, available land, and now a formal siting framework with the state. The May 2025 agreement with the Utah Office of Energy Development already set the stage for exploring a NuCube-supplied unit at this site, and this test campaign is the next operational step under that arrangement, not a standalone announcement.

Power availability as a leasing and site-selection variable

Utah's industrial market has already been shaped by power-hungry tenants, data centers and large manufacturers who ask about substation capacity and interconnection timelines before they ask about rent. A credible pathway to on-site or near-site nuclear heat and power, even years out, gives Utah another argument in competing with other states for these tenants. Owners of large industrial or flex parcels near existing energy infrastructure corridors should watch this story as a long-horizon factor in land value, the same way proximity to fiber or substations already shows up in pricing for data center sites.

Our clients at Team Utah Commercial (801-898-8810) field a steady stream of questions from industrial tenants and investors about power capacity constraints across Utah County and the broader Wasatch Front; this kind of technology, if it clears the regulatory bar, would be one more tool for sites that currently can't get the megawatts they need fast enough.

The gap between a test rig and a commercial unit

It's worth being precise about where this project sits. The 250 kW furnace tests are non-nuclear, meaning no fuel, no reactor operation, no licensing event triggered by this phase. The data generated here, combined with heat-pipe stress testing done previously with Canadian Nuclear Laboratories at temperatures above 900°C, is meant to build the technical safety case regulators will eventually review before any commercial unit gets licensed for public use. That's a multi-year process under the best of circumstances, and nothing in this announcement sets a commercial operating date for Utah.

For land owners and site selectors in Emery County and similar rural industrial markets, the right move isn't to price in a reactor. It's to note the state's continued willingness to position itself for next-generation energy infrastructure, and to watch whether NuCube's safety case clears its next regulatory milestone before assuming any of this shows up as a leasing amenity on a term sheet.
