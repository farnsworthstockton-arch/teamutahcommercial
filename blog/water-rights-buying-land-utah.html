Water Rights When Buying Land in Utah: What Buyers Need to Check

The rules in this guide come from the Utah Division of Water Rights pages and forms linked next to each one, all read on October 21, 2026. Water law is technical and every water right is different. Before you buy land that depends on water, have a water rights attorney, engineer or other qualified professional review the specific rights.

For land in Utah, water can decide what a property can actually be used for. A well, a ditch or a green field does not prove that water comes with the sale, and the state agency in charge of water rights warns buyers not to assume it does. Our due diligence checklist flags water rights as a separate item to verify. This guide goes deeper: what a Utah water right actually is, how it transfers, and what to check before you close.

What a Utah Water Right Is

The Utah Division of Water Rights says all waters in Utah are public property, and a water right is a right to divert water (remove it from its natural source) and put it to beneficial use (Division of Water Rights, Water Rights information). Utah classifies water rights as real property (Division FAQ).

Utah follows prior appropriation: use of water is based on seniority (who got there first) and on proven beneficial use. Every beneficial use, change or diversion of water in Utah must be authorized by the State Engineer, an office the Legislature created in 1897 (Division brochure, Buying, Selling & Transferring Water Rights in Utah, 7/1/2024).

Every water right file has the same basic parts. This table follows the Division's Water Rights 101 brochure (7/11/2024):

Element What it means
Water right number A two-digit area prefix plus a unique number (the brochure's example is 81-1250)
Quantity Measured in cubic feet per second (cfs) or acre-feet
Point of diversion Where the water is taken: a well, surface source, spring or drain
Place of use Where the water may be used, described by the public land survey system
Nature of use Irrigation, stock watering, domestic, municipal, industrial, mining and so on
Priority date The date by which the right is administered (its place in line)
Status Perfected rights are certificated, diligence claims or decrees; unperfected rights are pending approval or approved but not yet proven

An approved right becomes perfected when proof of beneficial use is filed and the State Engineer issues a Certificate of Appropriation (Division, Water Rights information). Until then it is a permission still in progress, so its deadlines matter to a buyer.

Land and Water Are Separate

This is the single most important point for buyers. The Division says plainly that a water right is not automatically attached to a property and that you should never assume a property automatically has water access (Buying, Selling & Transferring brochure). Title to a water right may be in a different name than the land where it is used, and a right can pass with the land, be conveyed in a specified amount, or be sold on its own (Division, Title information).

Water can also reach a property in ways that are not a state water right at all:

  • Water company shares. Shares in irrigation, canal and ditch companies are generally not transferred by deed. Each company has its own transfer procedure, usually the seller endorsing and delivering the stock certificate, and each company decides how much water a share delivers and what assessments it charges.
  • City or district service. If a city, water district or water company provides culinary (drinking) or outdoor water, the buyer should contact that provider to learn what is required to keep receiving service.

Both points come from the notes to the Division's Water Rights Addendum to Land Deeds form.

How Water Rights Change Hands

The Division says there are only two ways water right ownership is conveyed: by appurtenance (the right is attached to the land and passes with it) or by deed (the right is transferred much like real estate). For a deed to be effective, the Division lists two steps: complete a Report of Water Right Conveyance (ROC) with the Division, and complete the requirements at the county recorder's office where the water is used (Buying, Selling & Transferring brochure).

The county recorder holds the official title

The county recorder, not the Division, is the official office of record for water right title (Water Rights 101). The Division warns that its own records may or may not accurately reflect ownership, and that deeds should identify the water right numbers, any pending change applications, and the amounts and uses being transferred. If a deed does not mention the rights by number, appurtenance must be established with maps that only a Utah-licensed attorney, professional engineer, title insurance agent or professional land surveyor may submit (Division, Title information).

The Water Rights Addendum to Land Deeds

When land is deeded, the seller completes the Division's addendum and checks one box: all of the seller's water rights used on the parcel are conveyed, only a portion is conveyed, none are conveyed, or they are conveyed by separate deed. A disclosure section covers water company shares and culinary, outdoor or no water service. After recording, the county recorder sends a copy to the State Engineer; if the Division does not update ownership from it, the owner must file a Report of Water Right Conveyance under Utah Code 73-1-10(3). That filing is what updates the Division's records, lets the new owner file applications on the right and ensures the owner receives deadline notices. Applications already on file, such as change, extension or nonuse applications, transfer with the right (Water Rights Addendum to Land Deeds and notes). Conveyance reports usually take one to two weeks to process (Division FAQ).

Read this before you buy: The Division's own form warns that simply purchasing a water right does not guarantee that the right is in good standing, that the seller has clear title, that the Division will recognize the ownership change, or that the Division will approve any later change or extension. It encourages buyers to do proper due diligence on any water right before purchasing it (Water Rights Addendum to Land Deeds).

Use It or Risk Losing It

The Division says water rights that have not been exercised for a 7 year period are subject to forfeiture under Utah Code 73-1-4, although a state district court must declare the forfeiture. An owner facing unavoidable nonuse, such as during drought or legal proceedings, can file an Application for Nonuse of Water to protect the right (Division FAQ). For a buyer, that means asking how and when the water was last used, and asking for proof: crop records, meter readings, photos or water company records.

Open, Restricted and Closed Areas

Whether you can get new water depends on where the land is. The Division says you must own a water right to divert and use water in Utah, and that each area is open, restricted or closed to new water rights, which decides what kind of application you file (Division FAQ). In a closed area you need to acquire all or part of an existing water right and then file a change application to move or repurpose it.

An example close to the Wasatch Front: the Division's policy for Area 15 (Tooele and Rush Valleys) says Tooele Valley is closed to appropriations except for small amounts of shallow ground water, that surface waters are considered fully appropriated, and that on November 3, 2022, Governor Spencer Cox issued a proclamation suspending new appropriations of surplus and unappropriated waters in the Great Salt Lake Basin (Division, Area 15 policy, updated November 3, 2022).

Plan for time. Applications are advertised for 2 consecutive weeks, followed by a 20-day protest period and a decision by the State Engineer (Water Rights 101), and the Division says to allow several months between applying and a decision. Wells follow the same rules: a driller needs the State Engineer's permission before drilling (Division FAQ).

How Much Water Is in the Right?

When only part of a right is sold, the addendum describes the portion by its uses. The form's notes say domestic (indoor culinary) use is generally quantified at 0.45 acre-feet per family for a year-round residence and 0.25 acre-feet per family for a seasonal residence, and stock water at 0.028 acre-feet per equivalent livestock unit for full-year use. They also warn that the rights listed may not provide enough water for all historical uses (Addendum notes N4 and N5).

For commercial and industrial buyers, the nature of use matters as much as the quantity. A right approved for irrigation is not automatically usable for a warehouse or a plant; changing any characteristic of a right takes a change application (Water Rights 101).

When a City Asks for Your Water

If a city water system will serve your project, the city may ask for water rights as a condition of approval. For example, draft Tooele City Council resolutions in an August 2025 meeting packet state that Tooele City Code Chapter 7-26 governs the city's exaction of water rights as a condition of land use approval, with a fee in lieu allowed under a council policy (Tooele City Council meeting packet, August 20, 2025). Ask the city early what it will require.

A Buyer's Water Rights Checklist

  1. Get the list in writing: every water right number, any water company share certificates, and any culinary or outdoor water providers.
  2. Look up each right in the Division's Water Right Search: owner of record, status, quantity, uses, place of use and pending applications.
  3. Confirm title at the county recorder, the official record of title.
  4. Confirm recent use within the last 7 years, or any nonuse application on file.
  5. Check the area policy and whether your plans need a change application.
  6. Write it into the contract: which rights, how much water and which shares are included, with a due diligence period long enough to review them.
  7. Close it properly: record the deed with the Water Rights Addendum, file a Report of Water Right Conveyance if needed, and keep a current mailing address on file with the Division, as the addendum form requires.
  8. Get professional help. The Division recommends consulting its regional office before buying and seeking competent professional assistance if you are uncomfortable with the risk (Division FAQ).
