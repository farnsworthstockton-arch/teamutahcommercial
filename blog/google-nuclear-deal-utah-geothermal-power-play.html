Google's Nuclear Deal Mirrors Its Utah Geothermal Power Play

A Nuclear Deal in the East, a Signal for Utah

As first reported by Utah Money Watch, Google and Constellation Energy announced a 20-year power purchase agreement supporting more than $4.3 billion in upgrades across 11 nuclear units in Illinois, Pennsylvania, and New Jersey, adding 890 megawatts to the PJM grid. Constellation shares jumped 12.25% the day the deal was announced, a market reaction that had less to do with nuclear power itself than with what that 20-year commitment does to the financeability of new generation.

Utah brokers watching data center and industrial land shouldn't read this as an East Coast curiosity. The same mechanism — a hyperscale tenant committing decades of guaranteed payments so a developer can borrow against future revenue — is already running in Beaver County, where Google has a 396-megawatt power purchase agreement with Fervo Energy tied to the Cape Station geothermal project, with an option for roughly 600 additional megawatts. The first 33-megawatt block at Cape Station began commercial operation on September 30, 2026. That's not a proposal anymore. It's revenue.

Land Value Is Starting to Follow Financeable Power

For years, Utah industrial land was priced primarily on highway access, rail spur proximity, and entitlement timelines. Data center demand adds a fourth variable that increasingly dominates the other three: whether a parcel sits near power that a utility or developer can actually finance. An August 2026 Utah Legislature policy briefing put data centers under construction in the state at roughly 2,600 additional megawatts of demand, against average 2025 statewide demand of about 4,000 megawatts. That gap doesn't close with speculative generation. It closes with contracted generation, the kind Fervo now has at Cape Station and the kind Constellation just locked in with Google.

Beaver County land adjacent to Cape Station's build-out — 400 more megawatts under construction there for 2028 delivery — sits in a different category now than it did a year ago. Owners of ground near confirmed, revenue-generating power infrastructure have leverage they didn't have when geothermal capacity was still theoretical. Sellers and brokers representing those parcels should be pricing in the power story, not just the zoning.

SB 132 Is Already Writing the Lease Terms

Utah's S.B. 132, enacted in 2025, requires any customer needing 100 megawatts or more of new power to contract for its projected load and cover the incremental infrastructure cost itself, rather than passing it to existing ratepayers. That statute and the Google-Constellation deal are functionally the same instruction stated two different ways: if your business creates the load, you help finance the supply.

For commercial brokers structuring large industrial or data center leases in Utah, this changes the underwriting conversation with tenants well before square footage or NNN rates come up. A hyperscale tenant now has to show, often contractually, how its power will be sourced and paid for — information that used to live entirely with the utility. Deals that lack that clarity are going to move slower through entitlement and financing than deals that arrive with a signed power commitment in hand, which is exactly the gap Team Utah Commercial (801-898-8810) is seeing clients ask about when they evaluate industrial sites in Utah County and along the Wasatch Back.

Dates Worth Tracking

Two milestones deserve a place on any Utah CRE calendar tied to this story. The first is 2028, when Cape Station's additional 400 megawatts are expected online, a meaningful test of whether Beaver County geothermal can scale on the schedule its backers promised. The second is June 2030, the window by which Google's option on roughly 600 additional megawatts from Fervo could push the total commitment toward one gigawatt.

Neither date guarantees a Utah data center gets built on the back of that power. But both dates mark points where contracted megawatts either convert into built infrastructure or they don't — and Utah industrial land values near those projects will move accordingly, well ahead of any public announcement about a specific facility.
