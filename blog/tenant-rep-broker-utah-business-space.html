What a Tenant-Rep Broker Does for a Utah Business Looking for Space

When a business needs new space, the first instinct is often to call the number on the "For Lease" sign. That number usually belongs to the landlord's broker, who has agreed to represent the landlord. A tenant-rep broker (short for tenant-representation broker) is a licensed real estate professional who represents the business that is looking for space.

This guide explains what that representation means under Utah's real estate rules, walks through the leasing process step by step, and lists the questions to ask, both of the broker and of every space you consider. The legal points link to the Utah statute and rule we read on October 21, 2026. This is general information, not legal advice; your attorney should review any agreement before you sign it.

What "Tenant Rep" Means

In plain English, a tenant-rep broker works for you, the business, during a search for space. Their job is to help you work out what you need, find and compare spaces that fit, negotiate the business terms, and keep the deal moving until you have the keys. The landlord's broker is doing the same job for the landlord. Two brokers, two clients, one deal.

That matters because a lease is a long, expensive commitment that is hard to undo. Having someone on your side whose duties run to you, and not to the building owner, is the point of tenant representation.

What Utah's Rules Require of the Broker Who Represents You

  • A license. Utah's Real Estate Licensing and Practices Act makes it unlawful for a person who is not licensed under the act to engage in the business of, or act as, a principal broker, associate broker or sales agent with respect to real estate located in Utah (Utah Code 61-2f-201, version effective 5/6/2026). The act's definitions say "real estate" includes leaseholds, and a "transaction" includes a lease of real estate (Utah Code 61-2f-102, version effective 5/6/2026). You can look up any agent or brokerage by name, company or license number in the Utah Division of Real Estate's online license search.
  • A written agreement. Utah Administrative Code rule R162-2f-401a requires a licensee to define the scope of the agency in a written agency agreement with the principal, and the rule's list specifically includes "a tenant whom the individual represents."
  • Duties owed to you. The same rule lists the fiduciary duties a licensee owes the principal they represent: loyalty, meaning your best interests come above any other interest, including the agent's own; obedience to your lawful instructions; full disclosure of any material fact the agent learns about the other party or the transaction; confidentiality of information you give that would likely weaken your bargaining position, with exceptions for known material facts about a defect in the property or your ability to perform on the contract; reasonable care and diligence; and holding safe and accounting for any money or property entrusted to the agent.
  • Disclosure of who represents whom. Before a binding lease agreement is signed, the rule requires the licensee to disclose their agency relationships in writing. When the lease is signed, the licensee must confirm that earlier disclosure, either in the lease or in an attached document. The licensee must also give the party they represent copies of the lease, properly signed by each party.
  • Representing both sides takes your written consent. If one licensee represents both the landlord and the tenant as a "limited agent," the rule requires prior informed consent: a written explanation that each side may be represented by a separate agent and what information will be kept confidential or disclosed, plus a written acknowledgment from each party that it gives up the right to undivided loyalty, absolute confidentiality and full disclosure from the licensee, and understands the licensee will act in a neutral capacity.

Rule text is from the Cornell Legal Information Institute's reproduction of Utah Admin. Code R162-2f-401a, whose notes list the most recent amendment as effective 5/25/2022; read October 21, 2026. Check the official Utah Administrative Code for the current text.

The Process, Step by Step

  1. Define what you need. Before anyone looks at listings, write down your use, headcount today and what you expect in a few years, the type and size range of space, power, loading, parking, customer access, signs, your total budget, and your deadline. Check your current lease for its notice and renewal dates; they set your real timeline.
  2. Search the market. Your broker looks at available space that fits, contacts owners and landlord brokers, and narrows the field to a shortlist with the reasons each space made the cut.
  3. Tour. Visit the shortlist together and score every space against the same list of needs, so you compare like with like.
  4. Compare total cost, not just rent. Base rent is only part of what you pay. Many leases add operating expenses such as property taxes, insurance and common area maintenance, and the space may need build-out before you can move in. Our CRE Glossary explains terms like NNN, gross lease and tenant improvements.
  5. Request proposals and a letter of intent. A letter of intent (LOI) lays out the key business terms before the lease is drafted. It is typically non-binding, but it frames everything that follows, so treat it seriously.
  6. Check the space against the rules. The U.S. Small Business Administration advises that if you rent a property for your business, you should make sure it conforms to local zoning requirements, and notes that zoning laws are typically controlled at the local level (SBA, Launch your business: pick your business location; read October 21, 2026). Cities offer tools for this. Layton, for example, has an online Zoning Verification Letter Request, and St. George holds Development Staff Review meetings for potential projects. See our guides to Davis County and Layton industrial and flex space and the St. George and Washington County market for details.
  7. Negotiate the lease, and have your attorney review it. Your broker negotiates the business terms; your attorney reviews the legal document. Make sure every point you agreed to is written into the lease itself.
  8. Sign and move in. Confirm the agency disclosure appears in or with the lease, keep your signed copies, and line up permits, build-out, your business license and the move.
  9. Keep track of the dates. Put renewal option deadlines and the expiration date on your calendar the day you sign, so the next decision is yours to make on your schedule.

Questions to Ask Before You Hire a Tenant-Rep Broker

  • Are you licensed in Utah, and with which brokerage? (Then check it yourself in the Division of Real Estate's license search.)
  • Will you represent only my business in this search? If I like a space your own brokerage lists, how would you handle it, and what would you ask me to sign? (Remember that Utah requires written informed consent before one licensee acts as a limited agent for both sides.)
  • What exactly will our written agreement cover: which areas, which property types, how long it lasts, and how either of us can end it?
  • Who will do the work day to day, and how often will I hear from you?
  • What experience do you have with my type of space and my part of Utah? Can I talk with a past client?
  • How will you compare my options: rent alone, or total cost over the full lease term?
  • How will you keep my search confidential, for example from my current landlord?
  • What will you need from me, and by when?

Questions to Ask About Every Space

  • Does the zoning allow my exact use? Can I get that in writing from the city?
  • What business license, inspections and approvals will the city require before I open?
  • What build-out does the space need, and how does the proposed lease handle it?
  • What do I pay besides base rent, and how have those costs changed over the last few years?
  • Is there enough power, parking, loading and restroom capacity for my use?
  • What are the sign rules? Some cities require approval for every sign; Washington City, for example, says Planning and Zoning approval is required for all signage.
  • In Washington County, will my use need a new or larger water meter, and what will the water district charge for it?
  • Who maintains and replaces the roof, the heating and cooling system and the parking lot?
  • What happens later: renewal options, room to expand, the right to assign or sublease, and any early termination option?

Red Flags

  • Someone who will not give you their license details, or whose license you cannot find in the state's search.
  • A broker who will not tell you in writing whom they represent. Utah's rule requires written disclosure of agency relationships before a binding lease is signed.
  • Pressure to sign before you have confirmed that the zoning and licensing work for your use.
  • Promises that are not in the lease. If it matters to you, it belongs in writing.
