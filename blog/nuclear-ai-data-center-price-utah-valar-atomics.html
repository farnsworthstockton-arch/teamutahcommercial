Nuclear AI Data Center Near Price, Utah Clears First Land Vote

A Carbon County Land Vote With National-Scale Ambitions

The Utah Trust Lands Administration Board unanimously approved a lease of more than 640 acres of state land in Carbon County on Sept. 17, as first reported by KSL.com. Board members described the likely use as a data center. Separately, the Bureau of Land Management confirmed Valar Atomics has filed an initial application covering roughly 9,000 acres of adjacent federal land near Price, intended for a small gas-cooled nuclear reactor, a data center, and manufacturing facilities.

The scale under discussion is not modest. NPR's reporting on the project, called "Project Beehive," puts the design at 456 small reactors with a combined output of 9.6 gigawatts — a figure the outlet notes dwarfs Utah's current average energy output. Whatever the final build looks like, this is the biggest single land-use proposal Carbon County has fielded in a generation.

What This Does to Industrial Land Values Around Price

State trust land leases are priced to generate revenue for Utah's school trust fund, and a project of this size changes the math for every parcel within trucking distance. Water rights, substation capacity, rail access, and buildable acreage near Price will get repriced the moment speculative buyers start assuming a multi-billion-dollar tenant is coming. We've seen this pattern before in northern Utah County, where data center and manufacturing announcements pulled industrial land values up well ahead of any shovel in the ground.

Carbon County Commissioner Tony Martines framed the shift in energy identity bluntly at the September meeting, noting the county's history "from the coal oil to gas to solar now to nuclear." That's not just political messaging — it's a signal to anyone holding industrial-zoned acreage in the Price corridor that the county's economic base is actively being repositioned around energy infrastructure, and land that sat flat for years may not stay that way.

The Benefit-Sharing Question Investors Should Track

Lexi Tuddenham of the Health Environment Alliance of Utah raised the question that matters most for long-horizon investors: who actually captures the upside. Her concern wasn't abstract — it was about whether Utah, which absorbs the environmental and safety risk of hosting 456 reactors and nuclear waste storage, ends up with only a small slice of a multi-billion-dollar company's revenue. She put it directly: "We have a right to know as citizens what is going on."

That tension — risk borne locally, profit captured nationally — shows up in every large energy or data infrastructure deal, and it tends to determine whether a project gets fast-tracked or bogged down in years of review. Landowners and brokers positioning around this deal should watch whether Carbon County negotiates direct community benefit agreements, tax revenue sharing, or workforce commitments beyond the open houses Valar has already been running.

The Federal Review Timeline Is the Real Clock Here

State approval and federal approval are two different processes moving at two different speeds. The BLM has confirmed only that it received an application; if deemed complete, the agency still has to issue a Notice of Realty Action, run a full National Environmental Policy Act review, and hold public comment periods before any federal lease is finalized. That process can run years, not months, and it's the step most likely to reshape the project's footprint, water use, and reactor count before anything gets built.

For owners and tenants weighing industrial moves in the Price-Carbon County corridor, the state trust land vote is a strong early signal, not a finish line. At Team Utah Commercial we're tracking the BLM docket alongside county zoning and utility capacity filings, because the next twelve months of federal review will tell us more about final project scope than this month's state board vote did. Anyone with industrial-zoned land in eastern Utah should be asking their broker about substation proximity and water rights now, before the NEPA comment period turns a quiet rural parcel into a contested one.
