Utah Commercial Property Taxes Explained: How Values Are Set and How to Appeal

Property tax is one of the largest yearly costs of owning a commercial building or a piece of land, and in Utah the value behind that tax is set again every year. The system runs on a fixed calendar, and an owner who believes the county's value is too high has a written, step-by-step path to challenge it. This guide explains how Utah values commercial property, how the bill is figured, and how and when an owner can appeal.

Who Sets Your Property's Value

Most commercial real estate in Utah is locally assessed: the county assessor sets its value. The Utah State Tax Commission oversees local assessment statewide and directly appraises centrally assessed property, and it says questions about a specific bill or payment are handled by county officials (Utah State Tax Commission, Property Tax). Three county offices matter to you: the assessor sets the value, the auditor mails the valuation notice and serves as clerk of the Board of Equalization that hears appeals, and the treasurer mails the bill and collects the tax (Utah Property Tax Calendar; Tooele County Auditor).

January 1 Market Value

Utah values property as it stood on January 1, the lien date. The Tax Commission's calendar says all property, real and personal, is appraised based on its situs and status as of that date (Utah Property Tax Calendar, revised 02/14/2024), and in an appeal the parties present evidence of market value as of that lien date (Tax Commission, Appealing Locally Assessed Property).

Homeowners get a break that commercial owners do not. Utah allows a 45% exemption on the market value of qualifying primary residential property, and it applies only to primary residences (Tax Commission, Primary Residential Exemption). Office, retail and industrial buildings and vacant commercial land do not receive it.

Values are updated every year. Each assessor must keep a plan to update values annually and to review property characteristics at least once every five years (Utah Code 59-2-303.1, in the Property Tax Calendar). Tooele County, for example, says its 2026 values used 2025 market information and a new commercial land guide (Tooele County, Property Values and Taxes).

How the Tax Bill Is Figured

Your bill is your property's taxable value multiplied by a tax rate. Each taxing entity (county, city, school district, special districts) sets its own rate, and the combined area rate is the final rate the county charges against a specific property's assessed value (Tax Commission, Certified Tax Rates).

Utah's Truth in Taxation law controls how rates can rise. An entity that proposes a budget requiring a rate above its certified tax rate must give public notice and hold a truth-in-taxation hearing before adopting the increase (Utah Code 59-2-919; Tax Commission, Tax Increase Process). The Commission's history of the tax explains that the 1986 law required disclosure and hearings when an entity proposes to raise property tax revenue above what real growth on the tax base, such as new construction, brings in (Historical Overview of Utah's Property Tax). Your summer valuation notice lists budget hearings for fiscal-year entities that are raising taxes (Property Tax Calendar). A higher bill can come from a rate increase rather than a value increase, and only the value can be appealed.

Business Personal Property

Utah also taxes business personal property, such as equipment and furnishings, valued as of the same January 1 lien date. Small amounts are exempt. Tooele County's assessor, citing Utah Code 59-2-1115, says a business whose personal property has a total aggregate market value per county of $30,100 or less for 2026 qualifies for the exemption and need not file a signed statement in each continuing year it qualifies, while a business above that threshold must file. The same page says supplies became exempt in 2023, but expensed and capitalized items must still be reported (Tooele County Assessor, Personal Property). Note the earlier clock: personal property appeals are due May 15 in many cases (Property Tax Calendar).

Greenbelt Land and the Rollback Tax

Farmed land may be taxed under the Farmland Assessment Act, often called greenbelt, which assesses qualifying agricultural land on its productive capability instead of market value. The land generally must be at least five contiguous acres. When greenbelt land changes use, a rollback tax is due: the difference between the greenbelt taxes paid and the taxes that would have been paid at market value, for a maximum of five years before the change. A new owner who wants to keep greenbelt status must file a new application within 120 days of the ownership change (Tax Commission, Locally Assessed FAQ). If you are buying land to develop, your contract should say who pays any rollback tax.

How to Appeal, Step by Step

An appeal to the county Board of Equalization is about one thing: whether the county's market value is right. Salt Lake County, for example, says you may appeal only the total market value for the current year, and the Board adjusts only the valuation, not the tax dollars (Salt Lake County, Tax Administration Appeal).

  1. Watch for your valuation notice. The county auditor must mail the “Notice of Valuation and Tax Changes” on or before July 22 (Property Tax Calendar).
  2. Mark the deadline the day it arrives. Statewide, the appeal is due by the later of September 15 or 45 days after the notices are mailed (Property Tax Calendar). Salt Lake County accepts appeals from August 1 through September 15 and moves the deadline to the next business day when September 15 falls on a weekend or holiday (Salt Lake County). Tooele County's auditor says the 2026 deadline was Sept. 15, 2026 for most parcels and Aug. 1, 2026 for centrally assessed parcels (Tooele County Auditor, Board of Equalization).
  3. Put a number on it. Every valuation appeal must include the owner's own estimate of fair market value; the Tax Commission's history lists this among the 2024 changes (Historical Overview). The owner must also provide some evidence to support the lower value (Tax Commission).
  4. Bring income-property evidence. Salt Lake County says a rent roll and operating statement are the best source documents for income-producing property and that its appeal will not be accepted without the rent and expense information. It also accepts comparable sales (a minimum of three, up to five preferred, sold within one year before January 1 of the current year), settlement statements and independent fee appraisals. Appeals without evidence may be returned or dismissed (Salt Lake County).
  5. Get the decision. The Board decides within 60 days after the application is made (Property Tax Calendar). Know the risk: Salt Lake County says the value may be increased, decreased or left the same.
  6. Pay on time anyway. Salt Lake County says taxes are due by November 30 whether or not a decision has been received (Salt Lake County).
Centrally assessed property: If the Tax Commission, not the county, values your property, the appeal goes straight to the Commission by the later of August 1 or 90 days after the assessment is mailed (Property Tax Calendar).

Appealing to the State Tax Commission

If you disagree with the county board's decision, you can take it to the Utah State Tax Commission. The Commission describes the process this way (Tax Commission, Appealing Locally Assessed Property):

  • Deadline and form: file Form TC-194, “Request for Redetermination of County Board of Equalization Decision,” with the County Auditor within 30 days of the date on the county's decision. Count from the date printed on the decision, not the day it arrives.
  • First step: a Mediation Conference or an Initial Hearing, held by video unless a party asks to meet in person. Either side may skip mediation.
  • Second step: a Formal Hearing if mediation fails or a party disagrees with the Initial Hearing order.
  • Evidence: market value as of January 1, sent to the Commission and the county ten business days before the hearing, or it may not be considered.
  • Settlement: negotiation can continue at any point, and the Commission generally approves a value both sides agree on.

Missed the Deadline?

The Tax Commission says late appeals are accepted only under very limited criteria in statute and administrative rule (Tax Commission). As of this writing in October 2026, the 2026 window has closed in counties such as Tooele County. The practical move now is to get ready for next year: assemble a current rent roll, a twelve-month operating statement and any recent appraisal or sale information. If your tenants reimburse taxes under a net lease, also check what the lease says about who controls an appeal and who receives any refund. Buyers should ask for the last three years of tax bills, as our due diligence checklist recommends.
