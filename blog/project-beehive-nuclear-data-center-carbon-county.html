Nuclear Data Center Plan Puts Carbon County on Utah's CRE Map

A Data Center Story That Skips the Wasatch Front

As first reported by Construction Review, Valar Atomics has filed a federal proposal to build Project Beehive, a nuclear-powered data center campus spanning more than 9,000 acres of Bureau of Land Management ground near Price, plus roughly 650 acres of state land. The plan calls for about 456 small reactors generating a combined 9.6GW &mdash; more than double Utah's current average statewide generation.

Every major Utah data center conversation of the last few years has centered on the Wasatch Front and northern Utah County. This one doesn't. Price sits in Carbon County, a market that hasn't seen this kind of industrial capital attention in decades. If this moves forward even partially, it puts rural eastern Utah land, water rights, and labor into a conversation that's been exclusively a Salt Lake-to-Provo story.

Scale First, Construction Cost Second

The headline number people will repeat is the $1 billion Series B that Valar raised, led by Sequoia Capital, plus a $200 million credit facility. That's company financing, not a project budget &mdash; Valar hasn't disclosed what Project Beehive itself would cost to build. For brokers and lenders watching this site, that distinction matters. A well-capitalized developer is not the same as a fully-funded project, and 9,000 acres of reactors, fuel production, and waste storage is a different capital stack than a single data hall.

The proposed schedule has non-nuclear construction starting as soon as late 2026, first reactors operating in 2028, and full build-out targeted for 2032. That's an aggressive six-year arc for a project requiring federal environmental review, and it assumes the kind of phased reactor deployment &mdash; 25MW units built out incrementally &mdash; that lets Valar start generating revenue before the full 9.6GW is online.

NEPA Review Is the Real Clock Here

The Bureau of Land Management is still reviewing the application for completeness, and the project triggers a full National Environmental Policy Act review with public comment. Valar has been here before: the company previously pursued roughly 10,200 acres near Green River before abandoning that site. That history is worth sitting with. A company willing to walk away from one BLM parcel and pivot to another isn't locked into Price as the only option, and local opposition over nuclear waste storage and industrial use of public land could extend timelines well past 2028.

Separately, Utah's Trust Lands Administration has already approved a smaller, roughly one-square-mile lease tied to the project, projected to generate about $35 million in revenue over 50 years. That lease moved forward independent of the federal review, and it tells you how the state intends to monetize its trust land holdings around this kind of development regardless of how the larger federal approval plays out.

What Rural Land Owners Should Watch

Speculative land plays near announced mega-projects tend to run ahead of the underlying approvals, and Carbon County landowners, water rights holders, and anyone sitting on adjacent BLM-adjacent parcels should expect inbound interest well before a shovel goes into the ground. Valar's own public demonstration &mdash; powering a single Nvidia chip off its Ward 250 reactor in July, after achieving fueled criticality in June &mdash; shows the technology works at small scale, which is exactly why investors are paying attention to the land question now rather than waiting for 2028.

At Team Utah Commercial, we're already fielding questions from owners and investors trying to figure out whether this changes the calculus on industrial and rural land holdings outside the traditional data center corridor, and the honest answer right now is that it depends almost entirely on how the NEPA process unfolds over the next 12 to 18 months.

The Filing to Watch Next

The next meaningful signal won't be a press release &mdash; it'll be the BLM's completeness determination and the opening of the public comment period under NEPA. That filing will reveal how much of the 9,000-acre footprint is genuinely under consideration versus how much shifts, the way the Green River site did before it. Until that happens, Project Beehive is a serious, well-funded proposal rather than a confirmed construction timeline, and Utah's rural land market should treat it that way.
