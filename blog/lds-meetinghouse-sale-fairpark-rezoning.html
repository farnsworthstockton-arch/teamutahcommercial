LDS Church's $5M Fairpark Sale Reveals a Rezoning Premium

A Contract Above the List Price

As first reported by The Salt Lake Tribune, The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has a signed purchase and sale agreement to sell its meetinghouse at 660 N. Redwood Road in Salt Lake City's Fairpark neighborhood to developer Garbett Homes for $5 million. CBRE Group has the 3.5-acre parcel listed publicly at $3.1 million. That's an $1.9 million gap between the asking price and what's actually under contract, and it's tied directly to a rezoning petition that would let Garbett build 88 for-rent townhomes on the site.

The building, constructed in 1980, hosted two Tongan congregations that have since relocated to a facility on Star Crest Drive. Church spokesperson Chris Moore attributed the sale to the age of the structure. The site also carries the weight of tragedy: it's where two men, Sione Vatuvei and Vaea Tulikihihifo, died in a January shooting outside a funeral, a case that remains active against the man charged in connection with their deaths.

Pricing the Rezoning, Not the Building

A $3.1 million list price reflects what the land and a 46-year-old meetinghouse are worth as-is, under current institutional zoning. The $5 million contract price reflects what a buyer is willing to pay for the right to build 88 units on it, contingent on the city approving that upzone. Paul Garbett, who handles land acquisition for the company, told the Tribune, "things are in a bit of a holding pattern" while the deal and rezoning move through the approval process.

That holding pattern is the real story for anyone watching west-side land values. The church isn't selling a building at a discount to a developer who sees upside later — it's capturing a chunk of that upside in the contract price itself, with the rezoning approval as the closing contingency. Sellers of institutional-use parcels who don't structure deals this way are leaving money on the table, and buyers who do are betting the city council follows through.

A Pattern Worth Watching on the West Side

The Tribune notes this isn't an isolated sale. The church has sold several notable Salt Lake County properties in recent years as its membership base in the area has contracted, consolidating congregations into fewer, newer buildings. That leaves a scattering of large, well-located institutional parcels — often three to five acres, often sitting on arterial roads with decent access — that no longer pencil as meetinghouses but pencil very well as multifamily infill.

Fairpark, Rose Park, and Glendale sit on exactly that kind of inventory. Owners of similar aging, low-utilization religious or institutional parcels along the Wasatch Front should expect brokers and developers to start running the same math the church and Garbett just did — which is part of why ownership groups holding this type of real estate increasingly call firms like Team Utah Commercial (801-898-8810) before pricing a sale off a stale appraisal rather than off its entitlement potential.

The Product the City Wants

Garbett's plan leans toward family-sized renters: a mix of one-, two- and three-bedroom townhomes with attached garages, with roughly 30 of the 88 units carrying three bedrooms. That's a notably family-oriented unit mix for a for-rent product, and it's paired with a 10% rent discount for public servants and a stated intent to work with the city's Community Redevelopment Agency on affordability. Those commitments function as currency in the rezoning negotiation — the kind of concession a city planning commission wants to see attached to a petition before it signs off on density increases near the Jordan River.

Garbett's own filing frames the project in supply terms: "The project will bring additional housing units where none existed." That's a modest claim, but an accurate one for a parcel that's produced zero housing units for 46 years.

What Comes Next on Redwood Road

Nothing here is finalized. County land records still show the church as owner, the rezoning petition hasn't been decided, and Garbett has described the deal as sitting in a holding pattern. The company's own timeline points to a 2028 construction start, which leaves more than a year of entitlement work, city review, and a parallel riverside green-space partnership with the city still to be sorted out before dirt moves.

The number worth tracking is not the $5 million contract price but the city council vote on the rezoning petition itself. If Fairpark's upzone goes through cleanly, expect other owners of aging institutional parcels on Salt Lake's west side to test the same structure — list at current-use value, contract at upzoned value, and let the entitlement risk sit with the buyer until approval.
