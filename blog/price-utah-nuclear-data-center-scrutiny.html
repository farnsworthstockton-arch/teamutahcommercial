Price Utah Nuclear Data Center Faces Ethics, Permitting Scrutiny

A 9,000-Acre Bet on Carbon County

As first reported by Hoodline, Valar Atomics is pursuing a sprawling industrial campus on more than 9,000 acres of Bureau of Land Management ground in Carbon County, branded Project Beehive. The pitch combines small, gas-cooled reactors with a data center and manufacturing facilities — a federal-land scale of development that almost never comes up for sale through conventional channels.

That's the first thing brokers should register: this isn't a typical site-selection deal where a developer options private ground near rail and power. It's a federal lease process, which means the land economics, timeline, and risk profile look nothing like a standard industrial park transaction in Utah County or along the Wasatch Front. For landowners and municipalities near BLM acreage statewide, Price is becoming the test case for whether that model works.

The Permitting Gauntlet Isn't Close to Finished

Valar's own schedule called for nonnuclear construction to begin as early as the end of this year, with reactors online in 2028. That timeline runs into a federal process that hasn't started in earnest: BLM has said a Notice of Realty Action for the lease has to be issued, followed by full environmental review under NEPA and formal public comment. Valar's public affairs chief told the Salt Lake Tribune in October the company expected to request a conditional use permit from Carbon County within about a month — but as of early November, appearances before county and city bodies had not included public comment or questions.

That gap between promised transparency and actual practice is where the real risk sits for anyone underwriting a deal tied to this project — contractors, equipment suppliers, landowners hoping for spillover demand. A conditional use permit fight in a small county with an engaged, skeptical public can add months, and a NEPA review on a project this size rarely moves on a developer's preferred clock.

Federal Officials' AI Holdings Add a Layer of Risk

Legal experts told ProPublica that financial ties between Trump administration officials and AI companies raise ethical questions even as the administration pushes to expand data centers on public land. An analysis of Interior Secretary Doug Burgum's 2026 disclosure put his AI-company holdings between $18.1 million and $64.4 million; he resigned his positions at Arthur Ventures under his confirmation ethics agreement but kept the financial interests. Ethics counsel Cynthia Brown said Burgum's advocacy for data centers on federal land while holding those investments "could create exactly that appearance" of conflict. ProPublica also found 380 Trump appointees or their spouses hold Nvidia-linked financial interests.

None of that changes the legal status of a BLM lease application, but it does change the political weather around it. When the officials overseeing public-land data center policy have personal exposure to the sector's success, every permitting delay or denial becomes a story about motive, not just process — and that scrutiny slows decision-making even when nothing improper occurred.

Local Opposition Is Already Reshaping Outcomes

A Utah State University survey of nearly 900 residents found almost 76% oppose or strongly oppose building more data centers in the state, and 85% said a genuine public process matters to them. That sentiment isn't abstract: data-center backlash has already cost two Box Elder County commissioners their seats in 2026. Carbon County officials weighing Valar's conditional use permit request will be doing so with that precedent fresh in mind.

For brokers and owners tracking rural Utah industrial and energy-adjacent land, this is the variable worth watching closer than reactor technology or megawatt counts. Projects on this scale succeed or stall based on whether local government can hold a credible public hearing process — and whether the developer lets it happen before, not after, commitments are made. Our team fields a growing number of calls on BLM-adjacent and rural industrial parcels, and at Team Utah Commercial (801-898-8810) we're telling clients to underwrite permitting timeline risk as seriously as power availability.

Public Hearings Within Weeks Will Set the Real Timeline

Public hearings tied to Valar's conditional use permit request are likely in the coming weeks, and that process — not Valar's original end-of-year construction target — will be the real signal for anyone pricing risk on this project or ones like it. If Carbon County follows the Box Elder pattern of organized local pushback, a 2028 reactor timeline could slip considerably. If the county moves the permit through without the friction seen elsewhere, it becomes a template other rural counties will study the next time a federal-land energy or data-center proposal shows up on their agenda.
