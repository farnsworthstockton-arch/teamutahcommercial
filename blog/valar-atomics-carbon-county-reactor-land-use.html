Valar's Reactor Plan Tests Carbon County Land Use Rules

A Reactor Bet on Rural Carbon County

As first reported by the Salt Lake Tribune, Valar Atomics has spent the summer and fall briefing local officials in Carbon County on plans to build a network of small modular reactors about five miles north of Wellington, without yet opening the floor to public questions. The company has already leased a square mile of state land from the Utah Trust Lands Administration, a deal that runs for up to 100 years, and has applied to put hundreds of future reactors across 9,000 surrounding acres of Bureau of Land Management ground.

That combination of state trust land plus a BLM application is the part worth watching closely if you hold ground anywhere near that corridor. Trust lands exist to generate revenue for schools and institutions, so a century-long industrial lease on a square mile signals the state sees long-term value in that location — not just for Valar, but for whatever infrastructure follows a reactor cluster: transmission lines, service roads, water conveyance, and eventually the manufacturing or data center tenants the power is meant to serve.

The Permitting Timeline Shapes the Investment Horizon

Valar's own spokesman, Harry Hansen, told the Price City Council the full build-out is measured in decades: "That's 20, 30, 40, 50 years for the full build." The company plans to start on state land first while it waits years for BLM approval on the larger parcel, and a conditional use permit request to Carbon County is expected within the next month. That permit hearing — not this week's presentation — is the first real procedural checkpoint where land use, setbacks, and conditions get decided, and it's the first point at which neighboring owners get standing to comment.

For anyone underwriting land near the Wellington site, that timeline matters more than the reactor technology itself. A multi-decade build schedule means value appreciation tied to this project won't show up in a normal five- to ten-year hold. It favors patient capital, ground leases, and option structures over outright speculative purchases priced on near-term industrial demand.

Power, Water and Site Selection Pressure

Hansen described helium-cooled reactors paired with closed-loop cooling at data centers, aimed at avoiding heavy water draws — a detail that matters because water availability, not power, is often the binding constraint on industrial siting in rural Utah. If Valar can deliver dense, on-site power without competing for scarce water rights, that changes the calculus for data center and manufacturing site selection statewide, including markets well outside Carbon County that have been squeezed by grid interconnection queues.

Hansen also cited 70-decibel noise levels per reactor, comparable to a washing machine, but resident Kim Wilcox raised the obvious scaling question: Valar's stated plan involves more than 450 reactors on the BLM parcel alone. Noise, heat, and traffic studies for a single unit don't necessarily extrapolate cleanly to a fleet, and any conditional use permit process should force the company to address cumulative impact rather than per-unit figures.

Public Pushback as a Due Diligence Factor

Residents including Wilcox and physician Leo Hardy told the Tribune they didn't realize they had no formal path to comment at the Carbon County Commission or planning commission meetings where Valar presented. That frustration is now on the record before a single permit application has been filed, which raises the odds of a contested hearing once the conditional use request actually lands. For brokers and investors, a contested entitlement process is itself a risk factor — it can slow timelines further, add conditions on noise and buffer zones, and shape how adjacent parcels get valued or marketed in the interim.

We're already getting calls at Team Utah Commercial with REMAX Associates (801-898-8810) from owners near the Wellington corridor asking how a reactor cluster might affect grazing leases, mineral rights, and future resale value, and the honest answer right now is that nobody has firm numbers because the project hasn't cleared a single county hearing yet.

Watch the Carbon County Commission Calendar

The next concrete marker isn't a groundbreaking or an NRC license — it's whether Valar actually files its conditional use permit application in the coming weeks, and whether Carbon County schedules a hearing that lets Wilcox, Hardy, and their neighbors speak on the record. That hearing will produce the first official conditions tied to this project, from setback distances to monitoring requirements, and those conditions will do more to shape nearby land values than anything said in a City Hall lobby after the cameras leave.
