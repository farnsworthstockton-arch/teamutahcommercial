Utah Makes DOE's Short List for a Nuclear Innovation Campus

DOE Puts Utah on the Short List

In July, the U.S. Department of Energy named Utah one of five initial contenders to explore hosting a Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus, as noted in a Deseret News opinion piece by Sherrick Roanhorse of the Consumer Energy Alliance. DOE pulled from 28 applications across 26 states, so landing in the top five says something about the infrastructure and institutional case Utah was able to make, not just enthusiasm.

Utah's pitch centers on Tooele County, which the op-ed describes as an area with existing nuclear-related infrastructure and experience, and statewide Utah already has nuclear-related facilities, research capabilities and supply-chain businesses. That existing footprint is the whole argument: this isn't a state asking DOE to build from a blank field, it's a state asking DOE to build on top of what's already there.

Tooele County's Industrial Land Gets a New Pitch

The state has flagged a potential Tooele County corridor with transportation and transmission access. That phrase — transmission access — is the one owners of industrial and heavy-commercial parcels west of the Salt Lake Valley should underline. Power availability, not just road frontage, is becoming the scarce input that determines what a parcel can actually be built for.

The op-ed's author argues a full Innovation Campus could support thousands of jobs and attract major private investment if Utah advances past this stage.

5.6 Million People Need More Than Rooftop Solar

The source cites Utah's population climbing from nearly 3.6 million today to 5.6 million by 2065. The op-ed ties that growth to rising power demand, and for owners weighing power-hungry uses from cold storage to manufacturing to data infrastructure, Team Utah Commercial (801-898-8810) can help sort out which sites are positioned for it.

Advanced nuclear, including recycling technology that the piece notes could recover energy from the roughly 95% of potential fuel value remaining in used nuclear fuel, is being framed as one leg of a diversified energy stool rather than a replacement for anything. For tenants and developers evaluating sites today, the practical question is less about nuclear specifically and more about which corridors are positioning themselves now for the transmission capacity that will be contested territory in a decade.

The Next Milestone Is a Down-Select, Not a Groundbreaking

Utah is one of five contenders, not the chosen site. DOE's process still has to narrow that list, and the piece says safety and community involvement must remain at the center of any nuclear development.

Universities and workforce programs expanding nuclear curricula is a slower-moving but steadier signal than the site selection itself. Watch for DOE's next announcement on which contenders advance; that date, whenever it lands, will tell owners and investors in Tooele County whether this is a multi-decade infrastructure story or a one-cycle headline.
