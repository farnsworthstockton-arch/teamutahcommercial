Buying Your Business's Building With an SBA 504 Loan in Utah

If your business has been paying rent for years, owning the building can be appealing: you stop paying a landlord, you control the space, and you build equity. The U.S. Small Business Administration's 504 loan program is built for exactly this kind of purchase. Here is how it works, taken directly from the SBA's own rules, plus the steps for buying in Utah.

A note on sources. Every program rule in this article comes from SBA.gov: the SBA's 504 loans page, its CDC/504 loan program page, and its lending manual, SOP 50 10 8.1 (published September 25, 2026; effective October 1, 2026). All were read October 21, 2026. SBA updates these rules; confirm current terms with your lender before you rely on them.

What Is an SBA 504 Loan?

The SBA describes the 504 program as long-term, fixed rate financing for major fixed assets. It is an economic development program meant to create and preserve jobs and help small businesses grow. 504 loans are available only through Certified Development Companies (CDCs), which the SBA describes as its community-based nonprofit partners.

What it can pay for: buying land or buildings, building new, or renovating; long-term machinery and equipment with a useful remaining life of at least 10 years; and certain refinancing of qualified debt.

What it cannot pay for: working capital or inventory, and speculation or investment in rental real estate. In other words, this is a loan for a building your business will use, not a rental property.

Sources: SBA, 504 loans; SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, Section C. Read October 21, 2026.

How a 504 Deal Is Structured

A 504 purchase has three money sources. A bank (the SBA calls it the "Third Party Lender") makes a first loan. The CDC makes a second loan through a debenture that is 100% guaranteed by the SBA. You put in the rest. The standard shares, and the two cases that require more from you, are:

Share of project cost Standard New business or limited/special purpose property New business and limited/special purpose property
Bank (first lien) 50% 50% 50%
CDC / SBA (second lien) 40% 35% 30%
You 10% 15% 20%
Source: SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, Section C, "Typical 504 Structures" and "Borrower's Contribution," effective October 1, 2026; read October 21, 2026. See also SBA, CDC/504 loan program.

Your share is a minimum. SBA's manual calls these contributions the minimums required by policy, and a CDC may ask for more to offset weaknesses such as thin cash flow or limited working capital.

Two definitions from the same SBA manual matter here. A new business is one that has been in operation for 2 years or less when the loan is approved. A limited or special purpose property is a limited-market property with a unique design, special construction materials, or a layout that restricts it to the use it was built for. SBA's example list includes car washes, cold storage facilities where more than 50% of total square footage is equipped for refrigeration, gas stations, hotels and motels, hospitals and other medical facilities, and service centers with pits and in-ground lifts. SBA says the list is not all-inclusive, so ask your CDC early if your building is specialized.

Made-up example. A $2,000,000 building purchase by an established business in an ordinary building: bank $1,000,000 (50%), CDC/SBA $800,000 (40%), you $200,000 (10%). If the same buyer were a new business, the split would be $1,000,000 / $700,000 / $300,000. These are illustrations of the SBA percentages, not a quote; fees and closing costs are extra.

A few more structure details from the SBA manual:

  • Your contribution can include equity in land, buildings or equipment you already own that are part of the project.
  • Your contribution can be borrowed, but that loan must be subordinate to the bank loan and the 504 loan.
  • The program's administrative costs are not part of the project cost; they are added on top of the CDC loan amount.
  • The bank must be lined up when you apply, shown by a letter of intent, term sheet or commitment letter.
Source: SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, Section C and Appendix 3 (definitions), effective October 1, 2026; read October 21, 2026.

Loan Limits, Terms and Rates

  • Size of the CDC/SBA loan: at least $25,000. The total outstanding for one business, including its affiliates, is capped at $5,000,000, except that projects for small manufacturers (primary NAICS code in sectors 31, 32 or 33, with all production facilities in the U.S.) and eligible energy projects can go to $5,500,000 per project. (SBA's 504 page mentions both "up to $5 million" and "$5.5 million"; the lending manual's rule above is what separates them.) These limits apply to the CDC/SBA piece, not the bank loan.
  • Term: 10, 20 or 25 years, based on the useful life of what you are buying, with a maximum of 25 years for real estate. When the 504 loan is 20 or 25 years, the bank loan must run at least 10 years.
  • Rate: the CDC/SBA piece is fixed. Its rate is based on long-term government debt at the time the debenture is sold; the 10-year Treasury rate is most commonly the benchmark for 20- and 25-year debentures. The bank sets the terms of its own loan.
Sources: SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, Section C, "Debenture Limits," "Loan Maturities" and "Interest Rates"; SBA, 504 loans. Read October 21, 2026.

Who Qualifies

Under SBA's rules, the business applying must:

  • Be an operating business, organized for profit, and located in the United States. Nonprofits are not eligible (a for-profit subsidiary of a nonprofit may be).
  • Be small. Either it meets the SBA size standard for its industry, or it meets the alternative standard: tangible net worth of no more than $20 million, and average net income after Federal income taxes (excluding carry-over losses) of no more than $6.5 million for the 2 full fiscal years before applying. Affiliates count.
  • Meet the ownership rule. All direct and indirect owners, and SBA-required guarantors, must be U.S. Citizens or U.S. Nationals whose principal residence is in the United States, its territories or possessions.
  • Not be an ineligible type of business, and not be engaged in passive or speculative activities.
  • Meet a jobs or public policy goal. The standard test is that the project creates or retains one job opportunity per $95,000 guaranteed by SBA ($150,000 for small manufacturers and eligible energy projects). Projects can also qualify under other community development or public policy goals, subject to the CDC's portfolio average.
  • Show it can repay from the business's cash flow. SBA's manual says cash flow is the primary source of repayment, not collateral.
Sources: SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, Section A, Chapter 1 and Section C, Chapter 1, effective October 1, 2026; SBA, 504 loans. Read October 21, 2026.

The Owner-Occupancy Rule

This rule decides whether a building can work at all. Your business has to occupy most of it:

  • Existing building: your business must occupy 51% of the rentable property and may lease up to 49% to others.
  • New construction: your business must occupy 60%, may permanently lease up to 20%, and may temporarily lease another 20%, with the intention of using some of that extra 20% within 3 years and all of it within 10 years.

"Rentable property" means the total square footage used for business operations, excluding stairways, elevators and mechanical areas and including common areas. It can also include outdoor space your business actively uses (but not parking), such as outdoor storage yards for general contractors, trucking companies and moving and storage companies. If that describes your business, see our guide to industrial outdoor storage yards in Utah.

Made-up example. For a 20,000 square foot existing building, your business would need to occupy at least 10,200 square feet (51%) and could lease up to 9,800 square feet (49%). For a 20,000 square foot new building, you would occupy 12,000 square feet (60%), could permanently lease up to 4,000 (20%), and could temporarily lease another 4,000 (20%).
Source: SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, Section A, Chapter 3, "Occupancy and Leasing Requirements," effective October 1, 2026; read October 21, 2026.

Owning the Building in a Separate LLC

Some owners prefer to hold the real estate in its own company, separate from the operating business. SBA allows this through what it calls an Eligible Passive Company (EPC) that owns the property and leases it to the Operating Company (OC). The main conditions in SBA's manual:

  • The EPC leases 100% of the property to the operating company (which can sublease within the occupancy limits above).
  • There must be a written lease, subordinate to SBA's lien, with a term (including renewal options only the operating company can exercise) at least as long as the loan.
  • The rent cannot be more than the loan payments plus the EPC's direct costs of holding the property, such as maintenance, utilities, insurance and property taxes.
Source: SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, Section A, Chapter 2, "Eligible Passive Companies," effective October 1, 2026; read October 21, 2026.

Steps for a Utah Buyer

  1. Run the occupancy math first. Before you fall in love with a building, check that your business will use at least 51% of it (60% if you build).
  2. Talk to a bank and a CDC early. SBA keeps a list of Certified Development Companies you can filter to those serving Utah. The SBA's Utah District Office is at 125 S. State St., Suite 2227, Salt Lake City, UT 84138.
  3. Find the building. Browse current listings on our listings map. When you write an offer, give yourself enough time in the contract for SBA financing and due diligence.
  4. Do the due diligence the SBA will want. SBA requires an environmental investigation of commercial property pledged for the loan. If the property's current or known past uses match SBA's list of environmentally sensitive industries, it must start with a Phase I. Otherwise, for loans over $250,000 it starts, at a minimum, with an Environmental Questionnaire and a Records Search with Risk Assessment. Our Utah due diligence checklist and Utah zoning guide cover the rest.
  5. Understand the timing. SBA describes the 504 as permanent, "take-out" financing. An interim lender (the bank or another lender) provides financing between SBA approval and the debenture sale, and the debenture proceeds then repay the interim lender. Ask your CDC how your closing will be sequenced.
  6. Budget for owning. As the owner you pay the property taxes directly. Utah values commercial property at 100 percent of its fair market value as of January 1 each year.
Sources: SBA, List of Certified Development Companies; SBA, Utah District Office; SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, Section A, Chapter 5 (environmental) and Section C ("Interim Financing"); Utah State Tax Commission, 2024 Property Tax Annual Statistical Report (August 2025). All read October 21, 2026.

Buying vs. staying a tenant? If you lease today, compare what you pay now, including taxes, insurance and common area charges, with the cost of owning.
