Gross vs. NNN vs. Modified Gross: What a Utah Tenant Actually Pays

Two landlords quote you the same size space. One says the rent is a flat number per square foot. The other quotes a lower number and adds "plus NNN." The lower number is not always the cheaper deal. What you actually pay depends on who covers the building's property taxes, insurance and upkeep, and on how those costs change while you are in the space.

This guide is for Utah businesses leasing office, retail, flex or industrial space. If you are an investor buying a property with a triple net tenant in place, our NNN lease investing guide covers that side of the deal.

The Short Version

Lease type Who pays the building's taxes, insurance and common area costs What it means for your budget
Gross The landlord, out of the rent you pay. Easiest to budget. The rent is usually higher because it has to cover those costs.
Modified gross Split between you and the landlord. The lease spells out the split. In between. Read the split line by line; no two modified gross leases are the same.
NNN (triple net) You pay your share on top of base rent. Lowest headline rent, but the extra costs are yours and can change from year to year.
Sources: NAIOP / Commercial Real Estate Development Association, Development Magazine, "The Benefits and Risks of Triple Net Leases" (Summer 2017); LegalZoom, "What Is a Modified Gross Lease, and How Does It Work?" (updated January 13, 2026). Both read October 21, 2026.

What Each Lease Type Means

Gross lease

Under a gross lease, the landlord pays the building's operating expenses out of the rent tenants pay. In effect, the landlord is betting on what those costs will be over your term. If property taxes or insurance jump, that is the landlord's problem until your lease comes up for renewal. That protection is built into the price. Because a gross rent has to cover those costs, expect it to be quoted higher than a net rent for the same space.

A full service lease is a close cousin. Our CRE Glossary describes it as a lease where the landlord pays all operating expenses, including utilities, for one all-inclusive rent, and notes it is most common in multi-tenant office buildings.

NNN (triple net) lease

Under a triple net lease, you pay base rent plus your share of the building's operating costs: property taxes, building insurance, and common area maintenance (CAM). You pay those either directly or by reimbursing the landlord. Our glossary lists typical CAM items as landscaping, parking lot maintenance, snow removal, security and shared utilities, usually split by each tenant's share of the leased square footage.

Modified gross lease

A modified gross lease splits the operating expenses between landlord and tenant. A common version uses a base year or expense stop: the landlord covers operating costs up to a set level (the costs in the base year, or a fixed dollar amount per square foot), and you pay any increase above that level.

Sources: NAIOP / Development Magazine (Summer 2017) for gross and triple net; LegalZoom (updated January 13, 2026) for modified gross, base year and expense stop; Team Utah Commercial CRE Glossary for full service and CAM. All read October 21, 2026.

The Same Space, Three Ways (Worked Example)

Made-up example numbers. The figures below are invented to keep the math easy to follow. They are not Utah market rents or market expenses. Swap in the numbers from your own quotes.

Say you need 5,000 square feet and get three quotes. Rents are per square foot per year:

  • Gross: $24.00. The landlord pays taxes, insurance and CAM.
  • NNN: $18.00 base rent, plus the landlord's estimate of $5.50 for taxes, insurance and CAM.
  • Modified gross: $23.00, with a base-year stop. The landlord covers taxes, insurance and CAM up to this year's level of $5.50; you pay any increase above it.

Now suppose that in year two the building's taxes, insurance and CAM rise from $5.50 to $6.25 per square foot, and the base rents stay flat (real leases often add yearly rent increases; see "escalation clause" in our glossary).

The math: gross is 5,000 × $24.00. NNN is 5,000 × ($18.00 + $5.50) in year one and 5,000 × ($18.00 + $6.25) in year two. Modified gross is 5,000 × $23.00, plus 5,000 × the $0.75 increase ($3,750) in year two.

Three lessons from the made-up numbers:

  1. The lowest headline rent was not the lowest cost. The $18.00 NNN quote cost more than the $23.00 modified gross quote in both years.
  2. The order can flip when building costs move. In year one, NNN beat gross. In year two, it didn't. If costs had fallen instead, NNN would have gained.
  3. Compare totals over your whole term, not the per-foot number on page one of the proposal.

How Utah Property Taxes Reach Your Rent

Property tax is a pass-through the landlord does not control. Under a NNN lease you pay your share of it directly; under many modified gross leases you pay increases above the base year. Here is how Utah's system works, so a jump in your bill is not a surprise:

  • Commercial property is taxed on 100% of its value. The Utah State Tax Commission says all taxable property in Utah is valued at 100 percent of its fair market value as of January 1 each year. Primary residences get a 45% exemption; commercial property does not.
  • Values are mailed in summer. The county's initial valuation notice must be mailed to the property owner no later than July 22 of each tax year.
  • Only a short appeal window. Property owners may appeal the value to the County Board of Equalization; the deadline is generally September 15 for each tax year.
  • Bills come in November. In Salt Lake County, for example, tax notices must be mailed by November 1, and November 30 is the last day to pay current-year taxes without a penalty. Check your own county's dates.
Sources: Utah State Tax Commission, 2024 Property Tax Annual Statistical Report (published August 2025); Utah State Tax Commission, Appeals of Locally Assessed Property; Salt Lake County Treasurer, Dates & Deadlines. All read October 21, 2026.

What this means for you as a tenant: the Tax Commission describes valuation appeals as filed by property owners, but under a NNN lease you may be the one paying the tax. Ask for a lease clause that has the landlord send you each summer's valuation notice and appeal when the value looks high, or at least consider your written request to appeal. Also ask whether your monthly tax estimate is based on last year's bill. If it is, a higher November bill shows up later as a catch-up charge.

Costs That Can Land on You Under Any Lease Type

Even a gross lease rarely means "one check and nothing else." Budget for these no matter which lease type you sign:

  • Your own business personal property tax. Utah taxes the furniture, equipment and other tangible personal property your business owns, separately from the building. The Tax Commission says all tangible personal property is taxable unless exempted, and there is an exemption for property with a total aggregate fair market value of $30,100 (2026) or less per taxpayer within a single county. You have to apply for that exemption (see Publication 20, Rev. 02/26, read October 21, 2026).
  • Utilities and janitorial. Unless the lease is full service, ask who pays electricity, gas, water, trash and cleaning, and whether your space has its own meters.
  • Your own insurance. A building-insurance pass-through pays for the landlord's policy on the building. Check whether the lease also requires you to carry your own liability and contents insurance, and get a quote before you sign.
  • Repairs inside your space. Read the maintenance section line by line: who maintains and who replaces the HVAC units, overhead doors, plumbing, the roof and the parking lot.
  • Snow removal. Our glossary lists snow removal as a typical CAM item. In a Utah winter, ask how it is billed and what last winter's total was.
  • Build-out beyond the allowance. Ask in writing who pays if your improvements cost more than the landlord's tenant improvement allowance.

Questions to Ask Before You Sign

  1. What exactly is in CAM or "operating expenses"? Ask for the written list and the building's actual totals for the last two years, not just this year's estimate.
  2. Are big capital items excluded? A new roof or a rebuilt parking lot can be a large bill. Ask whether they are excluded or spread over their useful life.
  3. Is there a cap on increases? Tenants may ask for caps or limits on pass-throughs of the expenses the landlord can control, such as repairs and maintenance. Taxes and utilities are generally not part of that (source: NAIOP / Development Magazine, Summer 2017, read October 21, 2026).
  4. Is there a management or administrative fee on top of CAM? Ask how it is calculated.
  5. How are the extra costs billed? As a monthly estimate with a year-end true-up (reconciliation), or as actual bills? When will you get the reconciliation, and can you review the landlord's records?
  6. For modified gross: what is the base year or stop? Get the dollar figure in writing, and ask how it is adjusted if the building was not fully occupied in the base year.
  7. What is your share, and how was it measured? Your percentage of the building drives every pass-through.
  8. Will the landlord share the July valuation notice and appeal when the value looks high? (See the Utah property tax section above.)
