How Utah Property Tax Works

Three steps, in plain English. Open any heading to read more.

1. The county sets your property's market value as of January 1

Your county assessor puts a market value on your property each year, based on where it is and what it is like on January 1. Utah law says:

"All tangible taxable property located within the state shall be assessed and taxed at a uniform and equal rate on the basis of its fair market value, as valued on January 1, unless otherwise provided by law." (Utah Code 59-2-103(2), effective 7/1/2025)

The Tax Commission's 2025 annual report puts it this way: "Fair market value less any exemptions equals taxable value. Taxable value is the value against which the tax rate is applied to compute taxes charged." (2025 Property Tax Annual Statistical Report, page 2)

Sources: Utah Code 59-2-103 (effective 7/1/2025); Utah State Tax Commission, 2025 Property Tax Annual Statistical Report (dated August 2026). Read October 21, 2026.

2. Primary residences are taxed on 55% of value; commercial property on 100%

Utah gives a home that is someone's primary residence a "residential exemption equal to a 45% reduction in the value of the property" (Utah Code 59-2-103(3)). So a primary residence is taxed on only 55% of its market value. Everything else is taxed on 100%.

  • Taxed on 100% of market value: commercial, industrial, retail, office and vacant land (not farmland), plus residential property that is not anyone's primary residence, such as a vacation home.
  • Taxed on 55% of market value: primary residences, including apartments and rentals where a full-time tenant lives as their primary residence (Utah Code 59-2-103(6)(b)(ii)). The Tax Commission's 2025 annual report says primary residential property includes "condominiums, apartments and rental property, and includes up to one acre of land."
  • One acre limit: "No more than one acre of land per residential unit may qualify for the residential exemption" (Utah Code 59-2-103(5)). This estimator applies 55% to the whole value, so for a home on more than one acre its estimate may be low.
  • Part-year homes: a residence qualifies if it is used as a residence for 183 or more consecutive calendar days during the year (Utah Code 59-2-103(4)).
  • Rentals: the Tax Commission's Primary Residential Exemption page says the owner of a residential rental must submit an approved residential declaration.

Sources: Utah Code 59-2-103 (effective 7/1/2025); Utah State Tax Commission, 2025 Property Tax Annual Statistical Report, page 2 (dated August 2026); Tax Commission Primary Residential Exemption page (no date shown). Read October 21, 2026.

3. Taxable value times your tax area's rate

Every property in Utah sits in a tax area: the patch of map where the same set of taxing bodies (county, school district, city and special districts) overlap. Each tax area has one total rate, written as a decimal. For example, 0.010504 equals 1.0504% of taxable value.

Estimated tax = taxable value × tax rate

Your exact rate is on your valuation notice or tax bill. The Tax Commission also publishes every tax area's rate on its Tax Rates by Area page. On October 21, 2026, the newest file listed there was the 2025 Tax Rates by Tax Area (PDF). Each tax area has three rate columns; the actual rate is the last one, "Final Adopted Tax Rate".

Sources: Utah State Tax Commission, 2025 Tax Rates by Tax Area (Report 600, dated January 05, 2026) and Tax Rates by Area page; 2025 Property Tax Annual Statistical Report, page 3 (dated August 2026): "Tax rates are expressed as a percentage of taxable value." Read October 21, 2026.

Commercial vs. homes: the 2025 statewide numbers

Statewide in 2025, homes and commercial property paid about the same rate on their taxable value (0.94%). But because a home is taxed on only 55% of its value, its tax measured against full market value (the "effective" rate) was 0.52%, while commercial property's was 0.94%.

Appealing your value

You appeal your value to your county board of equalization. The deadline is "on or before the later of: (i) September 15 of the current calendar year; or (ii) the last day of a 45-day period beginning on the day on which the county auditor provides the notice" (Utah Code 59-2-1004(3)(a), effective 1/1/2026). Commercial owners follow the same rule.

County auditors must send valuation notices "On or before July 22 of each year", and for property the county assessor values, the notice must show "the deadline for the taxpayer to make an application to appeal" (Utah Code 59-2-919.1(1) and (2)(c)(iii)(B)). This page does not work out a date for you: use the deadline printed on your notice.

Your appeal must include "the taxpayer's estimate of the fair market value of the property and any evidence that may indicate that the assessed valuation of the taxpayer's property is improperly equalized with the assessed valuation of comparable properties" (Utah Code 59-2-1004(4)(a)(i)).

Sources: Utah Code 59-2-1004 (effective 1/1/2026) and 59-2-919.1 (effective 7/1/2026). Read October 21, 2026.

What this estimator does not cover
  • Farmland assessed under the Farmland Assessment Act.
  • Business personal property, such as equipment.
  • Rates adopted after 2025. On October 21, 2026, the newest by-tax-area rate file on the Tax Commission's Tax Rates by Area page was for 2025.
  • Other exemptions or credits you may qualify for.

Source for the newest rate file: Utah State Tax Commission, Tax Rates by Area page, read October 21, 2026.
